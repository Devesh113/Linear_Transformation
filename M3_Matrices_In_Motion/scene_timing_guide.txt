VOICE-OVER SCRIPT OUTLINE / CUES:
"Let's start with the foundation: what does a 2x2 matrix actually do?
 Mathematically, it's a function that takes an input vector and outputs a new vector.
 But where does it send them?
 Look at the two standard basis vectors: e₁, pointing one unit along the x-axis, and e₂, one unit along the y-axis.
 When matrix A acts on e₁, it extracts the first column: [a, c].
 When it acts on e₂, it extracts the second column: [b, d].
 This is the single most important rule in 2D linear algebra:
 The columns of a matrix tell you exactly where the basis vectors land."

"Now you might ask: why should we only care about e₁ and e₂? What about all the other vectors?

 A vector v = (x, y) is literally x steps along e₁, plus y steps along e₂.
 And because matrix transformations are linear, this relationship never breaks!
 A times v equals x times A e₁, plus y times A e₂.
 If you know where the basis vectors land, you know where EVERY point in the universe lands.
 Watch the grid: horizontal lines follow the new e₁, vertical lines follow the new e₂,

VOICE-OVER SCRIPT OUTLINE / CUES:
"Let's put this into practice with the key transformations from Lecture Sheet 10.
 First: A Shear. In matrix S, the second column is (0, 1)—so e₂ does not move at all!
 But e₁ shifts to (1, 1). The square skews into a parallelogram.
 Next: A Quarter-Turn Clockwise. Matrix R sends e₁ down to (0, -1), and e₂ right to (1, 0).
 Angles and lengths stay preserved; the origin stays locked.
 Third: A Pure Stretch. Matrix [[2, 0], [0, 1]] doubles the x-coordinate while leaving y untouched.
 Fourth: Reflection. Negating the second diagonal entry flips the vertical axis over the x-axis.
 And finally, the dramatic one: Collapse!
 Look at matrix C with all ones. e₁ lands at (1, 1), and e₂ lands at the exact same spot!
 Two independent directions have merged into one.
 The entire 2D plane loses a dimension and squashes onto a single line.
 Notice vector (1, -1): it collapses straight to the origin—a nonzero vector in the null space!"

SCENE 5: WHAT DOES THE TRANSFORMED SQUARE TELL US?
Timestamp: 2:55 — 3:35 (Duration: ~40 seconds)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
VISUAL EVENTS:
2:55 - 3:10 | Unit square corners tracked: 0, e₁, e₂, e₁+e₂  -->  0, Ae₁, Ae₂, Ae₁+Ae₂.
3:10 - 3:25 | Stretch matrix A = [[2, 0], [0, 1]]: Area doubles from 1 to 2.
             Calculation box: det(A) = (2)(1) - (0)(0) = 2. New Area = |det(A)| = 2.
3:25 - 3:35 | Collapse matrix C = [[1, 1], [1, 1]]: det(C) = 1(1) - 1(1) = 0.
             Area becomes exactly 0 as square flattens into a 1D line.

VOICE-OVER SCRIPT OUTLINE / CUES:
"Look closely at the unit square formed by e₁ and e₂. Its original area is exactly 1.
 After transformation, its new corners are 0, A e₁, A e₂, and their sum.
 The transformed square forms a parallelogram.
 How much did its area scale?
 That scaling factor is precisely what we call the DETERMINANT!
 For our stretch matrix, the determinant is 2, so the area doubles.
 But for our collapse matrix, det(C) = 1 times 1 minus 1 times 1, which is zero.
 An area of zero means our 2D shape was flattened into a line.
 The determinant tells you how much space grows, shrinks, or collapses."

SCENE 6: WHAT MAKES A TRANSFORMATION LINEAR?
Timestamp: 3:35 — 4:10 (Duration: ~35 seconds)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
VISUAL EVENTS:
3:35 - 3:45 | Linearity axioms: T(u + v) = T(u) + T(v) and T(c u) = c T(u).
3:45 - 3:55 | Vector addition: adding u and v before transformation vs adding T(u) + T(v) after.
             Parallelogram law preserved. Checkmark appears!
3:55 - 4:05 | Scalar multiplication: scaling vector by c=2 before vs after. Exact match!
4:05 - 4:10 | Key geometric signatures: Origin is fixed (T(0) = 0), straight lines stay straight,
             grid lines remain parallel and evenly spaced.

VOICE-OVER SCRIPT OUTLINE / CUES:
"Not all functions can be represented by a matrix. Only LINEAR transformations qualify.
 To be linear, a transformation must satisfy two core conditions:
 First, additivity: transforming a sum must equal the sum of the transformations.
 Second, homogeneity: scaling a vector before transformation must equal scaling it afterwards.
 Geometrically, this means two things:
 The origin never moves, and straight lines never bend into curves.
 Space can stretch, shear, rotate, or collapse—but the grid structure always remains uniform."
